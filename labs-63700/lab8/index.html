What This Lab Is About

In order to understand natural images, one needs to understand how objects reflect light and how humans perceive that reflected light as color. This lab introduces colorimetry, the quantitative measurement and manipulation of color. There are three major components to understanding color: how light is reflected from a natural scene, how humans perceive reflected light, and how a display, such as a monitor, produces light.

def load_spectra(path="data/data.mat"):
    """Return cmf (3, 31) with rows x0, y0, z0, and the (31,) illuminants S_d65 and S_fl."""

def load_rgb(path):

Step 2: Color Matching Functions and Illuminants

Reflection of light. The color that one sees is a function of both the reflectance of a surface and the spectral distribution of the light source, or illuminant. Let \(S(\lambda)\) be the spectral energy density of the illuminant at wavelength \(\lambda\), and let \(R(\lambda)\) be the fraction of that energy reflected toward the viewer. The viewer sees light with the spectral distribution

$$ I(\lambda) = R(\lambda) \, S(\lambda) \ . \tag{1} $$

So the perceived color of an object can depend on the illuminant. We are concerned with wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm, the range that is most visible. A simple model of an illuminant is the equal energy white \(S_{EE}(\lambda) = 1\). A more common model is the standard D65 illuminant, a tabulated set of measurements that approximates noon sunlight.

Human perception of color. Humans have three types of color receptors, or cones, called long, medium, and short for the wavelengths they are most sensitive to. Their measured responses \(l_0(\lambda)\), \(m_0(\lambda)\), and \(s_0(\lambda)\) are the cones' color matching functions. The total response of each cone is

$$ L = \int I(\lambda)\, l_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \ , \qquad M = \int I(\lambda)\, m_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \ , \qquad S = \int I(\lambda)\, s_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \ . \tag{2} $$

So while the spectrum of light may be very complex, the response of the visual system is limited to three dimensions. This is the tristimulus model of color vision. With the spectra sampled at \(\lambda = 400, 410, \dots, 700\) nm, the integrals become inner products,

$$ \begin{bmatrix} L \\ M \\ S \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} l_0 \\ m_0 \\ s_0 \end{bmatrix} I \ , \tag{3} $$

where \(l_0\), \(m_0\), and \(s_0\) are \(1 \times 31\) row vectors and \(I\) is a \(31 \times 1\) column vector.

The XYZ color space. The most standard representation of tristimulus values is the CIE 1931 \(XYZ\) system, related to \(LMS\) by

$$ \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix} = A \begin{bmatrix} L \\ M \\ S \end{bmatrix} \ , \qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{bmatrix} = A \begin{bmatrix} l_0 \\ m_0 \\ s_0 \end{bmatrix} \ , \tag{4} $$

where the inverse of \(A\) is approximately

$$ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2430 & 0.8560 & -0.0440 \\ -0.3910 & 1.1650 & 0.0870 \\ 0.0100 & -0.0080 & 0.5630 \end{bmatrix} \ . \tag{5} $$

The color of a spectrum \(I\) is then

$$ \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{bmatrix} I \ . \tag{6} $$

The functions \(x_0\), \(y_0\), and \(z_0\) are positive for all wavelengths. For the equal energy spectrum \(I_{EE} = [1, 1, \dots, 1]^t\), \((X, Y, Z) = (1, 1, 1)\), so each of \(x_0\), \(y_0\), and \(z_0\) sums to 1. The \(Y\) component is closely associated with perceived brightness and is called the luminance.

Chromaticity. Changes in the length of the vector \((X, Y, Z)\) change the brightness of the color. Changes in its direction change the hue and saturation, which together are the chromaticity. The chromaticity \((x, y, z)\) is \((X, Y, Z)\) scaled by its sum,

$$ x = \frac{X}{X+Y+Z} \ , \qquad y = \frac{Y}{X+Y+Z} \ , \qquad z = \frac{Z}{X+Y+Z} \ . \tag{7} $$

Geometrically, the chromaticity is the intersection of the vector \((X, Y, Z)\) with the plane \(X + Y + Z = 1\). Since \(x + y + z = 1\), often only \((x, y)\) is given.

Step 3: Chromaticity Diagram

A chromaticity diagram shows colors by their \((x, y)\) chromaticity. Combinations of any two colors always fall along the straight line between their two points.

The most saturated colors come from spectra with energy at a single wavelength. The \(XYZ\) coordinates of such a pure spectral source at wavelength \(\lambda_0\) are

$$ \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \int \delta(\lambda - \lambda_0)\, x_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \\ \int \delta(\lambda - \lambda_0)\, y_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \\ \int \delta(\lambda - \lambda_0)\, z_0(\lambda)\, d\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_0(\lambda_0) \\ y_0(\lambda_0) \\ z_0(\lambda_0) \end{bmatrix} \ . \tag{8} $$

Their chromaticities form a boundary for all visible colors. With the sampled data, they are the chromaticities of the 31 columns of the \((3, 31)\) array of color matching functions,

$$ (x, y, z)(\lambda_0) = \frac{\big(x_0(\lambda_0),\ y_0(\lambda_0),\ z_0(\lambda_0)\big)} {x_0(\lambda_0) + y_0(\lambda_0) + z_0(\lambda_0)} \ . \tag{9} $$

Standard RGB primaries. One important set of RGB primaries is specified in the CIE 1931 standard, with the chromaticities

$$ \begin{aligned} R_{CIE\_1931} &= (0.73467,\ 0.26533,\ 0.0) \\ G_{CIE\_1931} &= (0.27376,\ 0.71741,\ 0.00883) \\ B_{CIE\_1931} &= (0.16658,\ 0.00886,\ 0.82456) \ . \end{aligned} \tag{10} $$

These are useful for laboratory measurements, but not practical for displays. A more common set for displays is the Rec. 709 standard,

$$ \begin{aligned} R_{709} &= (0.640,\ 0.330,\ 0.030) \\ G_{709} &= (0.300,\ 0.600,\ 0.100) \\ B_{709} &= (0.150,\ 0.060,\ 0.790) \ . \end{aligned} \tag{11} $$

White points. A white point is the chromaticity of a “white light” spectrum. The D65 and equal energy white points are

$$ (x_{wp}, y_{wp}, z_{wp})_{D65} = (0.3127,\ 0.3290,\ 0.3583) \ , \tag{12} $$ $$ (x_{wp}, y_{wp}, z_{wp})_{EE} = (0.3333,\ 0.3333,\ 0.3333) \ . \tag{13} $$

Step 4: The Matrix from RGB to XYZ

Color primary transformations. Most displays produce colors by adding three primaries. Let \(\vec R\), \(\vec G\), and \(\vec B\) hold the \(XYZ\) values of the primaries. Then

$$ \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix} = r \vec R + g \vec G + b \vec B = M \begin{bmatrix} r \\ g \\ b \end{bmatrix} \ , \qquad M = \begin{bmatrix} X_r & X_g & X_b \\ Y_r & Y_g & Y_b \\ Z_r & Z_g & Z_b \end{bmatrix} \ , \tag{14} $$

and the \((r, g, b)\) needed for a color \((X, Y, Z)\) is

$$ \begin{bmatrix} r \\ g \\ b \end{bmatrix} = M^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \end{bmatrix} \ . \tag{15} $$

For almost all primaries, some visible colors give a negative \(r\), \(g\), or \(b\). A display can only produce positive quantities of each primary, so it cannot produce all visible colors.

Chromaticity and white point of the primaries. Primaries are usually specified by their chromaticities and a white point. The chromaticities give only the direction of each vector. The \(XYZ\) values are the chromaticities scaled by constants \(\kappa_r\), \(\kappa_g\), and \(\kappa_b\):

$$ M = \begin{bmatrix} x_r & x_g & x_b \\ y_r & y_g & y_b \\ z_r & z_g & z_b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \kappa_r & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \kappa_g & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \kappa_b \end{bmatrix} \ . \tag{16} $$

For a display, the white point is the chromaticity produced at \((r, g, b) = (1, 1, 1)\), or \((255, 255, 255)\) in 8 bits. (This definition differs slightly from the one in Step 3, but the same term is used for both.) Assume the brightest output has \(Y = 1\). Then the white point in \(XYZ\) is \((x_{wp}/y_{wp},\ 1,\ z_{wp}/y_{wp})\), and the constants are

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \kappa_r \\ \kappa_g \\ \kappa_b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_r & x_g & x_b \\ y_r & y_g & y_b \\ z_r & z_g & z_b \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} x_{wp}/y_{wp} \\ 1 \\ z_{wp}/y_{wp} \end{bmatrix} \ . \tag{17} $$

The rows of PRIMARIES_709 are the chromaticities of R, G, and B, so the matrix of chromaticities in equations (16) and (17) is its transpose.

You display a calibrated color image from a known illuminant spectrum and the reflectance at each pixel:

  1. Compute the reflected spectrum \(I\) of each pixel with equation (1), an \((H, W, 31)\) array.

  3. Transform each pixel to linear \((r, g, b)\) with equation (15) and \(M_{709\_D65}\).

$$ v = \mathrm{round}\left( 255 \cdot \mathrm{clip}(r, 0, 1)^{1/\gamma} \right) \ , \tag{18} $$

and the same for \(g\) and \(b\). Clip before the gamma correction.

The reflectances are scaled so that a white surface under D65 has \(Y \approx 1\), so the values of R are near 0.01. A white surface in this scaling has constant reflectance \(R(\lambda) = 1/Y_{65}\), where \(Y_{65} = y_0 \, S_{65}\) is the luminance of the D65 illuminant.

    """Map grid x = j * step, y = i * step (0 to 1), z = 1 - x - y to (r, g, b) = M^(-1) (x, y, z); set all three to 1 where any is negative.

Build the grid with np.meshgrid, so that \(x\) increases with the column and \(y\) with the row. A point where \(r\), \(g\), or \(b\) is negative cannot be produced by these primaries, and it is shown as white.
