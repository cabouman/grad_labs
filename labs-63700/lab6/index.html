def save_image(x, path):
    """Write an (H, W) uint8 array as an 8-bit grayscale PNG."""
    Image.fromarray(np.asarray(x, dtype=np.uint8)).save(path)


def to_uint8(y):
    """Round an array of gray levels, clip it to 0 to 255, and return uint8."""
    return np.clip(np.round(y), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)


def apply_table(x, table):
    """Apply a pointwise operation given as a table of 256 values: output = table[x]."""
    table = np.asarray(table)
    if table.shape != (256,):
        raise ValueError("table must have 256 entries")
    return table[x]

Step 2: Histograms

The histogram of a digital image shows the distribution of its pixel intensities. Let \(X_s\) be the pixel at position \(s\) in the lattice \(S\), taking the values \(0, \dots, L-1\) with \(L = 256\). The histogram of the image is

$$ h(i) = \sum_{s \in S} \delta(X_s - i) \ , \qquad i = 0, \dots, L-1 \ , \tag{1} $$

where \(\delta(0) = 1\) and \(\delta(k) = 0\) for \(k \neq 0\). So \(h(i)\) is the number of pixels that take on the value \(i\).

    """Return the (256,) int64 histogram h of a uint8 image; h[i] counts gray level i."""

Step 3: Histogram Equalization

The objective of histogram equalization is to transform the image so that the output histogram is uniform over the full range of gray values. Think of a pixel as a random variable \(X\) with cumulative distribution \(F_X(x) = P\{X \le x\}\), and form \(Y = F_X(X)\) by mapping \(X\) through its own cumulative distribution function. Then for \(0 \le y \le 1\)

$$ P\{Y \le y\} = P\{X \le F_X^{-1}(y)\} = F_X\big(F_X^{-1}(y)\big) = y \ , $$

so \(Y\) has a uniform distribution on \([0, 1]\). Therefore the histogram of an image can be equalized by mapping the pixels through their cumulative distribution function. We estimate it from the histogram by the empirical CDF,

$$ \hat F_X(i) = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i} h(j)}{\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} h(j)} \ , \qquad i = 0, \dots, L-1 \ . \tag{2} $$

The normalization term assures that \(\hat F_X(L-1) = 1\). However, \(Y_s = \hat F_X(X_s)\) may not extend over the full range of gray levels. To correct this, let \(Y_{\min}\) and \(Y_{\max}\) be the minimum and maximum of \(Y_s\) over the image, and form

$$ Z_s = (L-1) \, \frac{\hat F_X(X_s) - Y_{\min}}{Y_{\max} - Y_{\min}} \ , \tag{3} $$

rounded to the nearest integer. Histogram equalization is the transformation from \(X_s\) to \(Z_s\).

Step 4: Check the Equalization

A flat histogram has the empirical CDF \((i+1)/256\). The distance from flat is

$$ D = \max_{0 \le i \le 255} \left| \hat F(i) - \frac{i+1}{256} \right| \ . $$

Step 5: Contrast Stretching

Contrast stretching maps the darker pixels in \([0, T_1]\) to zero (black) and the lighter pixels in \([T_2, 255]\) to white. Then the pixels in \([T_1, T_2]\) are stretched out to use the full scale of \([0, 255]\):

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \le T_1 \\ 255 \, \dfrac{x - T_1}{T_2 - T_1} & T_1 < x < T_2 \\ 255 & x \ge T_2 \ , \end{cases} $$

rounded to the nearest integer.

The contrast stretching map.

If a pixel transformation is a one-to-one function \(y = f(x)\), then the input and output histograms are approximately related by

$$ h_{\text{out}}(y) \approx \left. \frac{h_{\text{in}}(x)}{|f'(x)|} \right|_{x = f^{-1}(y)} \ , \tag{4} $$

where \(f^{-1}(y)\) is rounded to the nearest integer. The map \(f\) is not one-to-one, so the output histogram may have spikes at 0 and 255. Between them \(f'\) is the constant \(255/(T_2 - T_1)\), so the output histogram should be a stretched and rescaled version of the input histogram.

Step 6: Gamma

For a long time the most widely used displays were cathode ray tubes (CRTs). In a CRT, the relationship between applied voltage \(V\) and light intensity \(I\) is a power law,

$$ I = V^{\gamma} \ . $$

The exponent is the gamma \(\gamma\) of the display, and a typical CRT gamma is around 2.5. LCD and OLED displays have very different physical properties, but they use correction tables to give a similar response. Most current displays follow the sRGB standard, which is close to \(\gamma = 2.2\). For an 8-bit pixel value \(x\), the light intensity \(y\) produced by the display is

$$ y = 255 \left( \frac{x}{255} \right)^{\gamma} \ . \tag{5} $$

Because of this power law, an image representing linearly scaled light intensity appears too dark on the display, especially for small and mid range pixel values. Gamma correction compensates by applying the inverse of equation (5), raising \(x/255\) to the power \(1/\gamma\), before display.

There is another important reason for a gamma corrected scale. The human visual system is more sensitive to changes in light intensity at low background illumination than at high. Quantizing a linearly scaled image to 8 bits produces easily perceived gray level steps in dark regions, called false contours. The gamma corrected scale very nearly matches a perceptually uniform scale. Therefore most digital cameras and scanners gamma correct images before storing them, and most digital images, such as JPEG and MPEG video, are already on a gamma corrected scale.

Step 7: Measure the Gamma of Your Display

The method visually compares a pattern of black and white lines to a fixed gray level. Viewed from a distance, the pattern appears as a uniform gray because of the blur of the optics of the eye. If half the lines are white, the perceived intensity of the pattern is

$$ I_c = \frac{I_{255} + 0}{2} \ , $$

where \(I_{255}\) is the intensity of level 255. By equation (5), gray level \(g\) produces \(I_g = I_{255} \, (g/255)^{\gamma}\). The two match when \(I_g = I_c\), which is when

$$ \left( \frac{g}{255} \right)^{\gamma} = \frac{1}{2} \ . \tag{6} $$
